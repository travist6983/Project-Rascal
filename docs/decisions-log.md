## Aug 18, 2026 — Phase 70: the Vellum wordmark, the logo kit, and the app icon

**Phase 47 said the icon decision was name-independent, and it was right.** *"The mark carries
no word, so it was decidable before the name was and would not have moved after it."* The name
moved twice — RASCAL to Dogear to Vellum — and the fan did not. What this phase added is one
change to it, not a redraw: the front card's entry title, a 138×15 bar standing in for a line of
type, becomes the actual line of type. The owner chose that over a wordless V-fan and a monogram.

**Both losing candidates are kept in the tree** as `vellum-icon-alt-v.svg` and
`vellum-icon-alt-monogram.svg`. A candidate nobody can look at again is a decision nobody can
revisit, and the V-fan in particular is the one to reach for if the icon ever has to work without
a name — which is the situation this project has now been in twice.

**The five cards are lifted out of `rascal-icon-fan.svg`, not redrawn.** The generator parses the
shipped file for each card's fill and the motif inside its clip, and re-authors only where the
cards sit. A redraw would have put a dozen changes on the table and called them one. **The parse
had a real bug in it**: the file lists cards in *drawing* order, back to front, with the front
card last at rotation 0, and reading that order as positional put the audio card in front. It
looked like a z-order problem. Cards are sorted by tilt now, and the sort carries the comment
saying why.

**The wordmark is drawn, not set.** A monolinear skeleton stroked at a constant weight with round
caps and joins, every curve a true circular arc. Three things follow: no font file ships, no
licence is anybody's to enforce, and the letters render identically in a browser that has never
heard of us. The round terminals are not taste — the app's type is SF Rounded and the cards carry
a 52/252 corner radius, and a flat-cut wordmark beside that art reads as a different company's.
Eight builds were drawn; the owner chose build 5, short ascenders, set lowercase. **The chosen
metrics are the generator's defaults**, so the lockups and the icon are the chosen build by
construction rather than by a number copied into three files by hand.

**Two defects that were invisible until the ground changed, which is the interesting part.**

The colour lockups painted the gap between one card and the next in canvas cream. That is correct
on the app's cream canvas and invisible there — and on the marketing site's white it reads as a
warm halo around every card, so the mark looks soft-edged where it should look cut. They knock the
gap out of the card behind now, which is true transparency, and the same file is right on white,
on cream and on ink. **Byte-identical on cream**, so nothing that was already right changed.

The one-colour lockup failed the same way from the other direction. Depth there cannot come from
tone, because there is only one tone; the first build used translucency, and overlapping sheets
compound and go grey the moment the ground is dark. It knocks out too.

**An asset that encodes an assumption about its surroundings looks correct until the surroundings
change.** Both of these were shipped-looking for hours.

**The hover component deliberately does the opposite, and that is a trade rather than an
inconsistency.** A knockout mask is built from where the cards *are*. The instant a card rotates
away, its notch stays behind and the mark tears — so the animated version strokes its gaps in
white, which moves with the card and costs it ground-independence. Motion and ground-independence
are opposite sides of one choice; the static files and the header component sit on opposite sides
on purpose. Written into both files, because it is exactly the difference a later reader corrects
into a bug.

> **Struck the next day, and the paragraph is kept rather than deleted because the mistake in it
> is the useful part.** *Motion and ground-independence are not opposite sides of one choice.*
> They were opposite sides of **a static mask**, which was the only kind that had been tried, and
> the write-up promoted an untested limit to a law. Give the mask's shapes the same class and the
> same custom properties as the cards they mirror and one CSS rule moves both: the knockout tracks
> the card and never has to know its ground. Corrected in phase 70a.
>
> **Applying it to the marketing site is what exposed it, and the site would have caught the stroke
> version in production.** That masthead is `color-mix(in srgb, canvas 88%, transparent)` over a
> `backdrop-filter`, so page content scrolls underneath and tints it continuously — no solid colour
> a stroke could use stays right at every scroll position. Cream is correct at the top of the page
> and a visible halo over a photograph twenty pixels later: a defect that appears only once
> somebody scrolls, which is the kind that ships.
>
> **The general form is worth more than the fix.** A limit found while building one thing is a
> property of that build until it has been attacked separately. Writing it down as a law makes it
> permanent, and *another agent recorded this one in the marketing repo's brand notes on the
> strength of this paragraph* — a false claim propagating across two repositories inside a day, on
> nothing but confident phrasing.
>
> **And it went both ways the same day, which is the part that makes it a mechanism rather than a
> lapse.** That agent gave a reason for keeping the site's footer tagline as text — that it was the
> only place the product name reached the Markdown twins — and it was invented; the twins are
> rendered from the page body and have never contained the footer. I wrote that reason into
> `footer.html` in good faith because it sounded like something already checked. So each of us
> planted a confident false reason in the other's repository, and **neither of us caught our own.
> Each caught the other's.** The advice was right in both cases; only the reasons were fabricated,
> which is what let them travel.
>
> **Both claims had the same tell: each one explained why something was impossible.** That is the
> class of statement that most deserves a second look and least often gets one, because it closes a
> question rather than opening one — nobody re-derives a dead end. Worth reading twice whenever a
> note says a thing cannot be done.

**The mask also had to be hung on an untransformed wrapper.** `maskUnits="userSpaceOnUse"`
resolves against the referencing element's own user space, so a mask on the rotated card group
interprets its coordinates inside that card's rotated frame. It renders as one solid silhouette
with no gaps at all, which looks like the mask being ignored rather than being applied in the
wrong place.

**Three cards, not five, below about 24px.** `vellum-mark-compact.svg` exists because the
five-card fan stops being five of anything at favicon size, and an honest three beats a smudge
claiming to be five.

**`index.html` is hand-kept and this generator does not write it.** `generate.mjs` next door
regenerates the contact sheet from its own variant list, and doing so once deleted the only page
that mentioned the shipped icon. Importing `generate-vellum.mjs` also writes nothing — the file
writes are behind an entry-point check, because the contact sheet's build imports a function from
it and a module that writes nine files as a side effect of `import` is one you cannot borrow from.

**Nothing has shipped to a phone.** `render-appicon.mjs` still reads `rascal-icon-fan.svg`, and
that file was not modified. Repointing it and re-rendering `AppIcon-1024.png` is an iOS-lane
change under the two-lane rule, and takes its own number. **The icon is drawn and decided, not
shipped**, and treating those as the same thing is how the PNG became a build product with no
build the first time.

**One thing the owner should see rather than have settled for him.** He confirmed the Vellum name
has been checked and is fine, and `decisions.md` open item 15 — filed the same night by another
session — records that no clearance search has been run. Both accounts now stand in the repo, as
disputed rather than as fact. **The chosen icon bakes the wordmark into pixels**, so the cost of
the name being wrong went up with this phase rather than down. That is an argument for settling
item 15, not for reopening the icon.

### Phase 66 — deleting an account (Aug 17, 2026)

- **A deletion request is stored with its own `execute_after`, not computed from a constant.** A
  request made under a seven-day policy runs on the terms it was made under, however the constant
  moves later. The alternative — `requested_at + CURRENT_POLICY` — silently re-dates every pending
  request the day somebody edits a number.
- **There is no `completed_at`, and the absence is the design.** A completed deletion has no row: it
  cascades away with the user, so a column recording completion could never hold a value.
  **Completion is the row being gone**, which is also the only record consistent with having been
  asked to forget somebody.
- **This is the one place in the service that hard-deletes user data, breaking rule 5 on purpose.**
  Soft deletes exist so a client can learn a row went away; there is no client left to tell, and a
  `deleted_at` on an erasure would be the service keeping exactly what it was asked to stop keeping.
  `CLAUDE.md` rule 5 now records the exception rather than being quietly violated by it.
- **`entries.author_id` became nullable with `ON DELETE SET NULL`** rather than cascading. A departing
  parent's entries belong to the child's archive, not to the parent — deleting an account must not
  delete the record of the childhood it was keeping. **This is a schema change made for a deletion
  feature that mostly matters to everything else.**
- **One pending request per user, by partial unique index** on `(user_id) WHERE cancelled_at IS NULL`.
  A plain unique would forbid asking again after a cancellation, which is a state a parent can
  genuinely reach.

### Phase 69 — the daily broadcast, with the send withheld (Aug 17, 2026)

- **`MARKETING_EMAIL_ENABLED` is deliberately separate from the mail credentials.** Configuring
  `MAIL_API_KEY` so that `POST /v1/subscribe` works must not also start mailing strangers. Two
  switches because they answer two questions, and conflating them is how a configuration step
  becomes a send.
- **The ledger holds no recipient, and a test asserts no column here is ever named for a person.**
  A per-subscriber table would be the mailing list in our database under another name, and the
  privacy page published the same day says we hold no copy.
- **The copy is generated to YAML rather than parsed at runtime, because `docs/` is not in the
  Docker image.** Fly builds from `api/` and the Dockerfile copies `src`, `alembic` and `seeds`, so
  a loader pointed at `../docs/` passes every test on a laptop and raises `FileNotFoundError` on the
  first scheduled run. **The failure mode is what made the decision, not the tidiness.**
- **One row per `send_date`, enforced by a unique index rather than by the job remembering.** A
  retried machine or a nervous hand-run reports the day already done and exits 0.

### Phase 75 — a test guard for the machine clock (Aug 17–18, 2026)

- **The guard is AST-based rather than textual, and that choice is the whole phase.** A grep over the
  source would flag the comment in `test_guardians.py` explaining why the offending call was removed
  — the explanation would be reported as the defect. **A rule that punishes writing down why is a
  rule people route around.**
- **`# machine-clock: <why>` is an earned escape hatch**, because a test genuinely about the runner's
  clock is legitimate and one using it as a stand-in for the child's date is a bug. The guard cannot
  tell those apart; a human writing a reason can.
- **It lives in `test_fixture_dates.py` beside the future-date guard** because it is the same
  argument: **a green run says nothing about which branch it took.** One file catches a date drifting
  toward the clock; the other catches a date read from the wrong clock entirely.
- **The bug it exists to prevent failed for four hours a day and passed the other twenty**, and every
  developer here runs US-Eastern where the two clocks never disagree — so it was invisible locally
  and got blamed on whatever had landed most recently, which was a rename that touched no scheduling
  code at all. **A test that is flaky by timezone reads as a flaky test, and the last commit takes
  the blame.**

- **Aug 19, 2026 (phase 97).** **The mission library is capped, counted and kept out of the model's context.** D7 keeps it out of anything public "whole or in generated slices" and calls it the one asset a competitor would want; a social generator that features prompts produces exactly those slices, publicly and permanently, one post at a time — the shape of leak nobody notices because no single post looks like a problem. Counted per distinct prompt rather than per post, and a discarded draft does not count. The alternative considered and rejected was trusting the operator: the risk is cumulative and invisible, which is precisely the kind a person cannot hold in their head.
- **Aug 19, 2026 (phase 97).** **Nothing in the studio posts anything.** No platform integration, no scheduler, no publish route. - **Aug 19, 2026 (phase 97).** Two Anthropic settings rather than one: `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` (`sk-ant-api…`, calls `/v1/messages`) and `ANTHROPIC_ADMIN_KEY` (`sk-ant-admin…`, reads organisation billing). ## Phase 127 — Sign in with Apple revocation (Aug 25, 2026)

- **The persist-vs-re-authorize fork is not a preference, it is arithmetic.** The preflight framed
  it as an owner decision. It is not: Apple's authorization code is single-use and expires in
  minutes, and the erasure runs seven days later in a job with nobody present. "Re-authorize at
  deletion time" therefore cannot reach the moment it needs to, and collapses into "store a
  long-lived token" — the same design, arrived at later. Persisting the refresh token also needs
  **no iOS change at all**, because the client has been sending the authorization code since day
  one and the server was discarding it in a comment that said so.

- **Revocation happens at execution, not at request time, and that is what makes the column
  necessary.** Revoking when the parent taps Delete would need no storage — the code is fresh
  right then. It would also mean anybody who changes their mind during the seven days has to
  re-consent to Apple, and whether Apple returns the same `sub` afterwards is **unverified**
  (`blocked.md` §11). Betting a family's entire archive on that assumption to avoid storing one
  string is the wrong trade, and it is the whole reason `credentials.apple_refresh_token` exists.

- **It is the first secret at rest in this database and the model comment says so under protest.**
  Every other long-lived token here is a SHA-256 — `refresh_tokens`, `email_sign_in_tokens`,
  `guardian_invites` — explicitly so that a database dump is not a set of working keys. This one
  cannot be: Apple's endpoint takes the token itself, not an identifier, so it must be replayable
  or it is useless. There is no encryption-at-rest helper in this repository and inventing one
  here would be a subsystem rather than a column. **Whether that column should be encrypted is a
  real open question and is left open rather than answered in a docstring.**

- **Apple's answer never decides whether an archive is destroyed.** Every failure mode — no key,
  an unsignable `.p8`, Apple unreachable, Apple refusing — logs at ERROR with the credential id
  and the deletion proceeds. A user who asked to be deleted and was not, because a vendor returned
  a 500, is a compliance failure with better logging. The exchange at sign-in fails in the
  opposite direction for the same reason: it must never turn a parent away from their own archive.

- **A1 and A3 were struck from the spec rather than built.** Deletion shipped in phases 66/67 and
  the `account-delete` handler in phase 53. Building `DELETE /v1/account` as specified would have
  put a `DELETE` meaning *erase* one path from a `DELETE` meaning *cancel*, and "make
  `account-delete` differ from `consent-revoked`" is the exact change `decisions.md:125` exists to
  prevent. **A spec describing shipped code is the thing to notice, not to implement.**

- **Question 5 is still unanswered and this phase did not answer it.** What survives an erasure for
  billing — today a `families` row, its `entitlements` row and every `app_store_transactions` row
  — is an accident nobody decided. Guard 5 stops the worst *consequence* of the residual without
  ruling on the residual itself; the proposed decision text is in the preflight report's §5 and
  remains unapplied, because a phase inventing an owner's decision is worse than a phase leaving
  it visible.

- **Three defects on the erasure path, found by reading rather than by the suite, none in scope.**
  Objects were destroyed before the only `commit()`, under a docstring claiming the opposite;
  `children.avatar_key` was never collected, so a child's face outlived their own erasure in the
  bucket; and the batch loop had no exception boundary, so one bad account discarded the totals
  for every account already committed. Fixed here because the phase was already rewriting the
  three lines around each of them and leaving a known erasure bug in place to keep a diff narrow
  is not a defensible trade on this particular path.

- **And one defect the fix introduced, caught by its own test.** The new rollback expires the
  session, so holding `DeletionRequest` instances across the loop made the next iteration's
  `request.user_id` a lazy load on an async session — `MissingGreenlet`, not a reload. The loop
  now carries ids and re-reads each row on the transaction about to act on it, which as a side
  effect makes a cancel arriving mid-run observable for the first time. **The test failed before
  the code did**, which is the only reason this is a paragraph and not a production incident.

- **The exchange is queued, not awaited, and finding out why cost two bugs.** A review caught it
  first: awaiting a round trip to `appleid.apple.com` inside the sign-in handler holds the
  request's Postgres transaction — and, on a first-ever sign-in, the uncommitted row lock on
  `uq_credentials_provider_subject_id` — open for as long as Apple takes to answer, on the first
  request the app makes on every launch. Moving it to a `BackgroundTasks` fixed that.

- **And the fix was silently inert, because FastAPI runs background tasks *before* the exit code
  of a dependency with `yield`.** `get_session` commits in that exit code, so when the task
  opened its own session the credential row this request had just written did not exist yet;
  `find_credential` returned `None` and the token was never stored. **Every failure on that path
  is deliberately non-fatal, so it would have failed silently forever** — no error, no log, a
  compliance feature that simply never worked. The remedy is one explicit `await session.commit()`
  before `background.add_task(...)`, and it is commented as load-bearing rather than tidying. It
  was measured, not assumed: a twenty-line FastAPI app printed the two events in order.
  **Only an end-to-end test through the route could see any of this**, which is the argument for
  writing one even when the service underneath has seventeen.

- **A second review round found the token could reach a log, and it is the finding this phase
  was most wrong about.** `session.flush()` was unguarded, and a `DBAPIError` renders the failing
  statement *and its bound parameters* into `str(exc)` — so a dropped connection mid-write would
  have put the live Apple credential into a structured log line, verbatim, under a message saying
  it had **not** been stored. Fixed twice over, deliberately: the flush is guarded (and rolls back,
  because otherwise the caller's `commit()` raises a `PendingRollbackError` that quotes the
  original exception — the same leak through a longer pipe), and `hide_parameters=True` is now set
  on the engine. **The second is the one that holds**, because it protects every statement that
  binds the column including ones nobody has written yet — the same reasoning `CredentialOut` uses
  for being an allowlist rather than an exclusion. The cost is that `[parameters: ...]` disappears
  from every SQLAlchemy error in the service; nothing sets `echo=`, so nothing was relying on it.

- **And the connection-held-across-a-vendor-call bug was fixed in one place and immediately
  recreated in another.** Moving the exchange into a background task took the held connection with
  it: `find_credential` ran *before* `_exchange`, so the task's own session sat
  idle-in-transaction for the whole round trip to Apple. Apple is now asked first and the database
  is not touched until there is a value worth writing. The deletion half of the same diff had it
  right from the start — commit, then revoke — which is what made the asymmetry visible.

- **Incomplete multipart uploads survived the erasure, and that is the same bug as `avatar_key`
  wearing different clothes.** Parts are not objects, so `delete_objects` against the key they
  will become succeeds and removes nothing; after the erasure the `media` row that named the
  upload has cascaded away and the parts are unreachable bytes of a child's video that outlived
  the request to destroy them. `s3.abort_multipart_uploads` is new and runs beside the object
  delete. **The filter is load-bearing and was verified rather than assumed**: `media.py:195`
  nulls `s3_upload_id` on completion, so a non-null one cannot be a finished upload — aborting one
  of those would destroy a real photograph.

- **A third round found a defect inside the second round's fix, and the test written to prove
  that fix was a false green.** The guard added above logged `credential.id` — but a failed flush
  rolls back, a rollback *expires* every object in the identity map including the primary key, and
  reading it afterwards is an implicit `SELECT` from a sync Session inside async code. That raises
  `MissingGreenlet` **from inside the handler meant to contain the failure**, so the warning never
  fired and the traceback escaped anyway. The id is now read before the flush.

- **Why the test could not see it, which is the more useful half.** It monkeypatched both `flush`
  and `rollback`, so nothing was ever expired. Replacing the fabricated exception with a real
  constraint violation still was not enough: a row added in the *same* transaction is **expunged**
  by the rollback and keeps its loaded `__dict__`, so the id read emits no SQL. Only a **committed**
  row is expired — which is the shape production always has, because the background task opens a
  fresh session and loads a row the request before it wrote. The test commits first now, and it
  fails against the unhoisted version. **Two false greens in a row on the same three lines**, each
  looking exactly like a passing test.

- **And `except ClientError` was the wrong net.** `BotoCoreError` is its *sibling*, not its parent,
  so every transport failure — a read timeout, an unreachable endpoint — escaped the per-upload
  guard and stranded every upload behind it, which is precisely what the docstring three lines
  above promised would not happen. Now `except (ClientError, BotoCoreError)`, with the loop itself
  under test for the first time: both existing callers monkeypatched the whole function away, so
  the loop, the guard and the counter had no coverage at all.

- **One docstring in this phase was confidently wrong, and it was one written to prevent a
  misunderstanding.** It claimed aborting a completed multipart upload "would destroy a real
  photograph" and that the `s3_upload_id` filter was the only thing preventing it. Both false — an
  upload id stops existing when the upload completes, so the abort is a `NoSuchUpload` no-op, and
  this same phase said so correctly in `s3.py`. **The correction matters because the false version
  read as a reason not to widen that query**, which is exactly what a future reader may need to do.

- **Thirteen mutations, thirteen failures.** Every fix in this phase was watched failing against a
  deliberately broken version before being believed, including the four above and the two the
  review found. One mutation initially "passed" and that was the mutation's fault, not the test's:
  `ruff` had collapsed the chained call onto one line so the patch string never matched. **A
  mutation that does not apply is a green result that means nothing**, which is worth writing down
  because it looks exactly like a test that works.

- **Nothing in this phase has spoken to Apple.** No Sign in with Apple key exists — `blocked.md`
  §24 is the errand — so every assertion is against a local `MockTransport`. What is proven is
  that the right request is built and that no failure of it can abort a deletion. What is not
  proven is that Apple accepts it, and that is a device check that stays in the runbook.
